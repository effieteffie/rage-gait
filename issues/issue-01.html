issue 1 / August 21

250-year-old capitalism still serves their slop the old fashioned way

until now, the flashy, mouth-watering images on the menus concealed the exploitation behind the scenes while slop has been in its every step.

“Slop” has immaculate phonetic, semantic and etymological harmony: The sibilant, slippery start convulses open to crown the vowel “o” before the stop consonant “p” builds and releases air pressure to plop out the word. These peristaltic, slimy and globular sensations parallel the semantic history of “slop” that connoted sludge, house waste, and pig food. In the 1400s, “slop” referred to “mudhole, puddle” or “dung” and “slime”, which reflects the unprocessed, spontaneous state of nature. In the 1650s, it referred to “semi-liquid food” before evolving to mean “refuse liquid of any kind, household liquid waste” in the 1800s, which is a shift that aligns with the rapid urbanization. Around the same time, “slop” started to refer to the mush and the scraps fed to livestock. Following this etymological course, it is no surprise that the contemporary usage of “slop” embodies the contradictions of moribund capitalism. Like its predecessor, today’s slop refers to the feed that provides enough sustenance and stimulation while extracting as much value from its subjects as possible.

Therefore, despite its digital sublime and unique timeliness, “AI slop” is not a novel anomaly but the contemporary crystallization of the good old Marxian commodity. Though, the pre-20th century “slop” implied a greater process it was a byproduct of, “AI slop” is not the repurposing of superfluous scraps but the intentional flattening and mushing of whole works into ready-made slop — slop for the sake of slop. The outputs are absurd but in no way alien; AI slop is how now “capital takes the shape of the specific means of production” (Marx, 1867, 47-48). Brainrot is not a corporate conspiracy or a sacrilegious strategy to unteach literacy or shorten attention spans but the logical course of capitalism once the scraps become more profitable than its whole. Why bother to process “raw material” with intent, finesse and meaning while you can churn and mass produce it into slop for a fraction of the cost? Why bother to keep your livestock pleased when you don’t need them satisfied but only plump?

Because this abjection is not situational but symptomatic of a totality, “slop” does not remain a matter of the phone and the scroll, but it seeps into the everyday until it replaces all that came before. Neoliberalism usually distorts the memory seamlessly, quietly, without being too much — which is why the definitive and decided disgust for AI-generated food I came across this week on Twitter struck me.

Beyond the hit tweets from my algorithmic bubble, research also suggests that AI-generated food images decrease not only the subjects’ willingness to consume the food but also how healthy, natural, and appealing they find the product to be (Stright et al., 2025, p. 1). And beyond the laboratory bubble, a real life case of a yet-to-open San Francisco business pointed to a visceral, actionable aversion from the public. After the Reddit users ripped apart their AI-generated menu — likening the items to a “loofah” and a “lung”, writing that the Frappuccino looked like it was wearing “fishnet stockings” — someone actually vandalized their restaurant by spraying “seriously” in graffiti (Yadegaran, 2026).

This disgust can be explained by the fact that these images are disgusting. But AI food especially bothers us because food is never about just food. While worries of putting food on the table or an obsession over what goes in and out of the mouth have always held importance, we now have a weighted, webbed and widespread “foodism” that “orchestrates experiences that are at once intensely individual and social.” (Probyn, 2003, p. 3). Sugary snacks fill one with guilt; the origins of a dish start international disputes; and the milk one chooses indicates their sexual orientation — food is how we become human and constrain ourselves within the human. The reason why our means of sustenance became our medium for self-actualization is what Marx calls the “law of estrangement”:

According to the economic laws the estrangement of the worker in his object is expressed thus: the more the worker produces, the less he has to consume; the more values he creates, the more valueless, the more unworthy he becomes; the better formed his product, the more deformed becomes the worker; the more civilized his object, the more barbarous becomes the worker; the more powerful labor becomes, the more powerless becomes the worker; the more ingenious labor becomes, the less ingenious becomes the worker and the more he becomes nature’s slave. (“Estranged Labour, Marx, 1844”)

As the result of his estrangement, the worker “only feels himself freely active in his animal functions — eating, drinking, procreating, or at most in his dwelling (…)” (Ibid). Therefore, eating is not only compulsory but compensatory. To make up for the body’s alienation at work, we stretch ordering food into a routine that involves all functions: A call to the libido through “food porn”, sacred dwelling in flipping through the menu and fantasizing about their order, and the bodily satiation and fullness finalizing the ritual. Thus, menu-slop is not the annoying yet scrollable brainrot but the disruption of the sacred animal functions that allow workers to be human — it is their subordination, starvation and castration. The machine that supersedes them at work now bleeds into their off-clock jouissance, reminding the viewer that they might be next in line to lose their livelihood to the machine that was built off the labor of the people it replaced.

Even the cozy pretense of the “small business” vanishes the moment the petit bourgeois can cut off costs by exploiting these artists; even the passionate, community-oriented food truck owner degrades their own work by allowing the machine to depict it. This degeneration and degradation of the self-perpetuating slop, slop for the sake of slop, is no unordinary betrayal or new financial feature — it is the very essence of capitalism. As Engels writes, expecting any different method or product from it would be “the same as expecting the electrodes of a battery not to decompose acidulated water, not to liberate oxygen at the positive, hydrogen at the negative pole, so long as they are connected with the battery.” (Engels, 2010, p. 114).

Human-made menus derived profit by putting thought and effort into presentation, proving human to human communication to be central, ever-present despite corporate interventions and alienation. Thus, despite its fraudulent representation of the food item, AI-generated menu images are surprisingly crude signifiers of the mode of production behind them. By removing the “aura of inevitability and authority” in the touch of the people who painted capitalism grace, the “synthetic aesthetic culture” of AI provides the “most naked representation of capitalism” to date (Markelj and Kemper, p. 4-5). Unlike the humans it steals from, the machine cannot conceal the exploitation slop behind the scenes: Patented seeds distributed by monopolies that can bankrupt farmers for growing their own food, harvests stripped of nutritional value to transfer monetary value to the shareholders, surplus value stolen from the workers to pay them with the scraps, the slop of the earnings — eventually the morally and materially inferior food item in your hands that is only the fraction of what it actually could have been. AI slop is not an anomaly, but yet another layer peeled off the cast of capitalism — in its trickery, realer than it has ever been.
