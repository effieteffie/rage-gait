250-year-old slop is still served the old fashioned way

“Slop” has immaculate phonetic, semantic and etymological harmony: The sibilant, slippery start crowns the vowel “o” before the stop consonant “p” plops out the word. These peristaltic and globular sensations parallel the semantic history of “slop” that connoted sludge in the 1400s, household waste in the 1800s, livestock food in the 19th century, and sentimental or low quality works from the 20th century and on. Following this etymological course of “slop” corresponding to the conditions and consequences of productive forces from medieval to modern times, it is no surprise that the contemporary usage of “slop” embodies the contradictions of contemporary capitalism.

While the pre-20th century “slop” implied a greater process it was a byproduct of, “AI slop” is not the repurposing of superfluous scraps but the intentional flattening and mushing of whole works into optimized content that serves “superficial competence”. Despite the “asymmetrical effort” that goes into creating and mass distributing endless possibilities of customization and personalization, most AI-slop is not only tolerated but even enjoyed (Kommers et al., 2026). These affective, psychological, and creative registers are thoroughly discussed in media and communication studies, as AI-slop is a digital-native trend that evolves and multiplies through algorithmic optimization. However, when AI-slop crosses the digital line, when its slop-form is frozen in a tangible artifact that is used in everyday scripts and schemas, it evokes significant outrage from people.

Beyond the hit tweets from my algorithmic bubble, research also suggests that AI-generated food images decrease not only the subjects’ willingness to consume the food but also how healthy, natural, and appealing they find the product to be (Stright et al., 2025, p. 1). And beyond the laboratory bubble, a real life case of a yet-to-open San Francisco business pointed to a visceral, actionable aversion from the public. After the Reddit users ripped apart their AI-generated menu — likening the items to a “loofah” and a “lung”, writing that the Frappuccino looked like it was wearing “fishnet stockings” — someone actually vandalized their restaurant by spraying “seriously” in graffiti (Yadegaran, 2026).

This disgust can be explained by the fact that these images are disgusting. But AI food especially bothers us because food is never about just food. While worries of putting food on the table or an obsession over what goes in and out of the mouth have always held importance, we now have a weighted, webbed and widespread “foodism” that “orchestrates experiences that are at once intensely individual and social.” (Probyn, 2003, p. 3). Sugary snacks fill one with guilt; the origins of a dish start international disputes; and the milk one chooses indicates their sexual orientation — food is how we become human and constraint ourselves within the human. The unified outrage is due to not the compulsory but the compensatory function of food, as the worker “only feels himself freely active in his animal functions — eating, drinking, procreating, or at most in his dwelling” (Marx, 1844).

To make up for the body’s alienation at work, ordering food is stretched into a routine that involves these functions: A call to the libido through “food porn,” flipping through the menu and fantasizing about their order, and the bodily satiation and fullness finalizing the act. Thus, menu-slop is not the annoying yet scrollable brainrot but the disruption of the sacred animal functions that allow workers to be human — it is their subordination, starvation and castration. The machine that supersedes them at work now bleeds into their off-clock jouissance, reminding the viewer that they might be next in line to lose their livelihood to the machine that was built off the labor of the people it replaced.

As seen in the swift adaptation of these menus, the cozy pretense of the “small business” vanishes the moment the petit bourgeois can cut off costs. Even the passionate, community-oriented food truck owner degrades their own work by allowing the machine to depict it. This degeneration and degradation of the self-perpetuating slop, slop for the sake of slop, is no unordinary betrayal or new financial feature — it is the very essence of capitalism. As Engels writes, expecting any different method or product from it would be “the same as expecting the electrodes of a battery not to decompose acidulated water, not to liberate oxygen at the positive, hydrogen at the negative pole, so long as they are connected with the battery.” (Engels, 2010, p. 114).

Human-made menus derived profit by putting thought and effort into presentation, proving human to human communication to be central, ever-present despite corporate interventions and alienation. Thus, despite its fraudulent representation of the food item, AI-generated menu images are surprisingly crude signifiers of the mode of production behind them. By removing the “aura of inevitability and authority” in the touch of the people who painted capitalism grace, the “synthetic aesthetic culture” of AI provides the “most naked representation of capitalism” to date (Markelj and Kemper, p. 4-5). Unlike the humans it steals from, the machine cannot conceal the exploitation slop behind the scenes: Patented seeds distributed by monopolies that can bankrupt farmers for growing their own food, harvests stripped of nutritional value to transfer monetary value to the shareholders, surplus value stolen from the workers to pay them with the scraps, the slop of the earnings — eventually the morally and materially inferior food item in your hands that is only the fraction of what it actually could have been. AI slop is not an anomaly, but yet another layer peeled off the cast of capitalism — in its trickery, realer than it has ever been.
