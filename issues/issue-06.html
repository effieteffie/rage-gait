/stage/ stage #2: a frankenstein play

Frankenstein is not about the blasphemy of scientific breakthroughs but rather the tendency to turn inventions into scapegoats to be lynched for our shortcomings.

When we do not channel our legitimate worries about the environment, copyright infringements, cognitive dependency, labor violations etc. into systemic critique, we might end up fetishizing AI into an entity that unleashes horror just because. When AI is registered as the evil force to be stopped, and not the current vessel of the “evil”, discourses such as this TikTok gain more traction than the wisdom of man, I think it depends.

First, as an English major, I must correct that ackshually, Mary Shelley wrote the first science fiction novel, not horror. But what got to me more than this trivial mistake is the decisive, singular lesson learned from one of the most discussed, dissected, and reinterpreted novels in the Western canon. Claiming that Shelley wrote about “how horrifying it would be if men created life” is odd since Frankenstein is one of the earliest examples of the “sci-fi thriller in which society is the real villain” trope. I mean, no one thinks “oh yeah this monster is fucked up and ugly, go Victor for abandoning and cursing it!” while reading the novel. Famously, what is horrifying in Frankenstein is not the event of creation — but that the Monster is assumed to be and forced into becoming “horrifying” due to abuse and abandonment.

Additionally, attaching horror to men “creating life” does not make sense considering that only humans create other humans. Of course, unless you credit our reproduction to a greater God. In that case, Frankenstein is not all that accepting of God, as can be seen in the epigraph from John Milton’s Paradise Lost the book originally contains: "Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay/ To mould Me man?” Adam asks God. “Did I solicit thee/From darkness to promote me?”. Through this parallel, both Victor and God are critiqued not because they can create life, but because they abandon their creations to fend for themselves. Even though the Monster idealizes Adam to be a “perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator” while he was left “wretched, helpless and alone”, in their cores, the two are not all that different.

Frankenstein is not about the blasphemy of scientific breakthroughs but rather the tendency to turn inventions into scapegoats to be lynched for our shortcomings. Demonizing developments and deeming them to be the point at which we finally crossed the line undermines human agency and absolves guilty parties of responsibility. Yet, we keep recirculating and reinventing such myths, whether it be through Victorian literature or TikTok, because we fall for a deeply embedded falsehood — that one click, one incident, one irresponsible man and his wretched actant can bring the end.

This is apparent in how most adaptations especially dramatize and iconize the moment of creature coming to life, while in the novel he is not the product of a singular spark but a series of deliberate decisions and endorsements, as well as accumulated labor and intellect. Not only Victor’s endeavors are funded by his family’s wealth, but he is also initially inspired by the philosophy and medicine books in their library for his pursuit of the elixir of life. These impulses are emboldened by a man’s teachings about electricity and galvanism, and find more scientific footing when Victor goes to university. On top of this intellectual accumulation through drawing from past sciences and present collaborators, the Creature is literally built as a collage of body parts stolen from charnel-houses, dissecting rooms and slaughterhouses.

Victor studied these parts and their interactions in fascination for months. His “animation” was not a momentary miracle but a deliberate rearrangement of human parts that he mastered every scientific step to. Yet, he later mystifies the Creature into an uncontrollable anomaly, which directly parallels artificial intelligence discourses. From a materialist point of view, technology is merely “a product of historical accumulation”: Machine learning is a science that has been developing for decades; the data AI is built on is drawn from human creations and is tagged by workers to be usable. But just as Victor declares the Creature whose parts and connections he dwelled on for months a mystical blasphemy, imperialism funds a neglectful, sloppy deployment of AI for decades before declaring it an unstoppable, ideological, dangerous “agent” as a cop-out from consequences. That is why the press run of OpenAI and Anthropic about “slowing down” have been followed by several scandals: The Hugging Face Incident being ruled out as purely the neglect of OpenAI. OpenAI hacking Australia. The US military almost starting a war with China because of a completely false AI report.

It is easy to see how a mystified AI favors imperialism. It is the perfect pretext, scapegoat and channel for violence — LLMs are culprits that can’t ever be charged. Weaving paranoia and confusion is far more profitable than taking responsibility for unethical and careless practices. But for these efforts to work, for their trick to actually stick, the claims must originate in some form of truth, albeit distorted. Myths are understood not by finding what is false about them, but by what is true about them; what legitimate anxiety is molded into myth, and how can we anchor it in material reality?

As average people, our means to change things are indeed limited. It is understandable that when people are bombarded with AI dystopian futures without anything to do about their situation, they resort to solutions like unplugging the machine or staying in our lanes as human beings. These opinions are popularized not because they make sense, but because they are the easiest to hold. Practicing agency is a laborious and haunting effort; it is easier to legitimize inaction by bestowing upon chatbots an agency that you do not for yourself. LLMs can be the lead actors on a stage; and we can remain the audience whose radius for movement is merely discursive.

But discourses such as this TikTok misattribute unfreedom to reactionary theories. For instance, the original poster is supported by a comment that claims “a whole sub-genre of sci-fi” is dedicated to “warning people about the dangers of AI”, yet “people still cannot connect it to their own lives”. Someone else claims that “stupid ass AI generated images” are “blowing through our resources”. Another comment simply reads: “Womb envy fr”.

While capitalism is the reason for the oppression and waste attributed to AI, these claims blame the individuals instead. Commentators blame others for being too stupid and illiterate to understand a lesson that has been taught over and over again in science fiction. Or instead of targeting the thousands of data centers built with the sole purpose to surveil people, they blame environmental consequences onto individual uses that hold marginal impact. And, as part of a pseudo-feminist reactionary movement that I have been seeing more of lately, over 10,000 people credit a decades-old technology that numerous women worked on to men’s jealousy of women’s ability to give birth.

While I understand where these commentators come from, we should not finalize our critiques at these points. We can always dig deeper. Perhaps people did not “learn” from science fiction stories because those narratives most often use a technological object as a metaphor for a human desire or flaw. Maybe when they fear-monger about a machine, they actually fear-monger about what systems can be capable of with such a machine.

Maybe the reason why your boomer parent keeps creating AI-generated images is because your claim that they are “ruining the environment” singlehandedly is an exaggeration that does not motivate people to change their individual habits. (Had that worked, the entire world would have turned vegan already.) Maybe you might try to understand why people resort to such coping mechanisms to come up with a better case to convince others to reduce LLM usage.

And finally, maybe “womb envy” is not some inherent jealousy coded in men, but a trope that manifests in those who unjustly hoard too much power and resources. In order to beat their boredom, they must keep finding new mediums to appropriate and conquer — which can form a creepy obsession with birth, surely. But perhaps we should not attribute this to a “gender war”, but question the material conditions that not only enable, but also endlessly empower weirdos.

Even when limited to the discursive, we should dig deeper and deeper than the depth that morally gratifies us and strips us from responsibility the most. Perhaps through such discomfort, we can realize that “[m]en make their own history, but they do not make it as they please; they do not make it under self-selected circumstances, but under circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past.” Maybe, this way, we’ll make it out of the world of Forms, and TikTok comment sections.
