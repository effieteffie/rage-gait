issue 5 / September 18

/stage/ stage #1: a play of two powers

Just as the channels through which capital moves and the number of people on each end narrow, AI neither comes to life nor is laid to rest — haunting the narrative as specter and spectacle regardless.

Drawing from Lenin’s Imperialism: the Highest Stage of Capitalism, I described artificial intelligence as the /stage/ stage of capitalism due to its self-explanatory, self-perpetuating and self-deconstructive qualities. If I try to argue this by pointing at different dimensions and trends, I might come off like that one guy connecting red lines on a whiteboard. So, instead of attempting an all absorbing and awfully abstract mapping, I wanted to start by analyzing the tiniest and most literal unit of this analogy — the very stage itself.

For this, I picked two moments. One is from Sam Altman’s presentation at the 2025 OpenAI DevDay; the other is from AUTOMATA: WHO AM I, a recent theatrical collaboration of Shanghai Theatre Academy and Jushen Technology. These two could not be further apart in size, signaled importance, and overall semiotics. One is a presentation that communicates the model’s improvements to an audience (most importantly, to the shareholders). The other is an “Interactive Robot Launch Show” that tackles complexities of human-machine symbiosis both textually and technically. One is literal and uncomplicated as the subject directly speaks to an audience, while the other is unabashedly abstract and artsy.

Though, these different contexts do not make one stage any less constructed than the other. From the broad daylight reflecting on the screen, the wooden rostrum and the planter trough adorned with greenery to the simple, unassuming attire worn by a billionaire representing a company worth a trillion, Altman’s scene persists to be seen as laidback and natural. Approachable, even. Though, my favorite bit of the composition exposes the artifice in the insistent candidness, which is the placement of the icon closest to Altman’s head that floats like a speech bubble. There, Altman is no different than a character who voices what he must voice, not unlike the latter actor absorbed into the scene, standing next to the very embodiment of artifice.

If they are both staged on their own terms, where else do they two differ? Their core difference can be observed through their one similarity, which is that both are events in which a couple of people take stage and a large crowd watches their performances. This asymmetry in who dictates and who is dictated to is natural in most planned events; but the scale at which this trend reproduces is how the highest stage of capitalism compares to the infantile stage of socialism.

While the OpenAI event is one of the many that centers the development and deployment of AI in the US, it is also representative of the rest. There is little diversity in content or the characters of these forums — most AI-defining moments are of a couple of guys on a stage speculating about fates they will unleash but not suffer. Whether it be about marginal developments that are nothing to the public but a mating dance to the investors, or dystopian imaginaries about how people will buy their intelligence from them in the future — their performances are not in tune with reality. They are only continuous claims that beg for more financial capital without delivering productive returns or tangible benefits to others.

This is not a conspiracy or a big plot, but a consistent character of how capital runs the US. The trend resembles all that came before starting from industrial phases to the more recent bubble of NFTs. But the disparity between how much money is thrown into AI versus how much profit it can actually bring is absurd in unseen rates before. Its frontier accumulation relies on computational infrastructure, which grabs money by the promise of becoming the best and returning immense revenue to its investors. The promise cannot be fulfilled because of the promise itself — these products can always, therefore should, get better. Monopolies mean that the “accumulation regime” that is built on this basis cannot ever “demonstrate its capacity to valorize the capital it commands”. Just as the channels through which capital moves and the number of people on each end narrow, AI neither comes to life nor is laid to its rest — haunting the narrative as specter and spectacle regardless.

This abstraction is how early capitalism progressed now to its moribund stage. Just as value degraded into fiction that is sold to those it is extracted from, material reality came to be defined by the same ideologies it gave birth to. That is why Altman’s stage consists of words and icons that insist upon themselves. He does not have to contribute anything to the world; he must only convince you that he will. Hell, he does not even have to convince you — he must only convince his investors that he will convince you somehow. That is why the promises read “[h]igher intelligence, [i]mproved instruction following, [b]etter experience” in a plain font without bothering with any of the grandiose and excitement of previous technology presentations. It only has to be better in some aspects than before — keep weaving the fiction it is the reference for.

Through myths the ruling class fetishizes and mystifies the very product they created, and begs to be absolved of its consequences. That is the reason for all the noise about AI becoming conscious or bots threatening to take over the world you might have heard this week. Just like the floating writings that do not have to materialize to steal your very material money, fear-mongering does not have to be based in reality to work. The “sci-fi distractions” are to take the focus from actual problems varying from intellectual theft in unseen scales to data centers poisoning communities. Instead of focusing inward to plan how to reconstruct their models and infrastructure, they must instead write open letters that mention China 10 times.

To see through their unfounded claims about China straight from the “Cold War playbook”, we must return to the stage in Shanghai.

Although this image is much more abstract than the Altman one, it captures a more realized instance of AI. Here, unlike the floating texts or unfounded promises, AI is materialized in a humanoid form. Because AI investments do not have to maximize profits by taking on mostly the software focused products, creators can experiment in humane and artistic curiosities. This product, therefore, is the staging of a year-long research focused on how to reflect human emotion through robot movements, and its reflection in play. In spite of the abstract aura of the image, AI here is not mere discourse or a premise, but is standing right there in the flesh – or silicon.

This variety in play and experimentation is how AI-related existential anxieties do not turn into myths or cults, but can be confronted head-on. The image shows that in its form, AI is not that scary at all. Because AI does not have to serve as a fear-mongering myth or a job-stealing threat, the human and humanoid figure can turn to one another not in antagonism, but in collaboration. The robot is not there to “replace the actor”, but only to diversify his performance; it is not there to take over, but to explore a “carbon based and silicon-based coexistence”. This is what makes their flashing silhouettes turned to one another special: It reflects the continuity inherent to dialectical materialism. The human and his creation do not fear extinction or dominance over one another, but their interactions continuously challenge and improve one another.

This staging is not the singular form for discussion and deployment of AI. The asymmetrical decisive power between the playwright and the audience is not the summary of a singular mode that is like Altman’s — it is only a singular example of how AI is explored and applied everyday in China. It is far from being the only performance based event, not even nearing the attention humanoid olympics or fairs got. Of course, how fast a humanoid can run is entertaining to watch, but it also attests to how efficient the technology will be in rescue missions. Watching these bots perform martial arts or choreography is entertaining, while they also train nuances and flexibility of micro movements necessary in daily chores and services to help humans.

This inherent human-centric approach is embedded in socialism with Chinese characteristics. Because it is not capital but the people running the country, AI strategies do not operate through absorbent bubbles but “national orchestration and provincial competition”. They are not limited to abstract and capital-chasing missions, but those “targeting diffusion of AI across manufacturing, administration, and infrastructure”. Therefore, the benefits are observed not only on stage but also in real life, especially in agriculture, medicine and education: Farmers do not have to only follow tradition but can utilize tangible data and information to care for their crops. Pharmacists are relieved of the physical tolls such as having to navigate the inventories, or loading and unloading vials. Implementations in schools help teachers monitor progress and improve interactions with students instead of giving them anxiety about getting replaced.

Considering these differences, one can understand why 84% of Chinese people are excited about AI compared to only 38% of Americans. Considering the roots of these differences being one of socialism versus capitalism, one can understand why Amodei calls AI a “technological miracle” in the language of abstracting and dissipating imperialism, while Marxist Chinese scholars recognize it as the “objectification of human essential forces” at the current point in dialectical, material history.

Because this self-replicating yet self-cannibalizing “AI bubble” cannot go much longer, the US companies must spend billions to convince the masses that when AI is “[l]eft unchecked, it could outrun our ability to understand and control these systems”, whereas dialectical materialism always “adhere[s] to the unity of technical rationality”, and acknowledges AI to be “developed, designed, and manufactured by humans” to improve real life.

The fear of Marxism-Leninism gaining momentum is why the US must sanction, bully, and demonize China. Language of “authoritarian power” and “regime” must be deployed to distract from the very fact that socialism not only works, but it actually excels despite the impossibilities imposed upon it. And that is why they do not have to engage in smear campaigns or imaginary races when they can simply do nothing and win.
