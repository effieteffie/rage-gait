issue 2 / August 28

niu l(ai) (2026) and historical amnesia

Disclaimer: I could only watch a bootleg of the film with unofficial subtitles, and I had to rely on auto-translation for a lot of the materials I quoted. My twice removed viewing and researching of the film means this newsletter is prone to have mistakes in it, but that is okay. Niu Lai wouldn’t want me to do it any other way.

Digital sublime refers to the collective amazements and fears that new digital technologies evoke in society. As Mosco traces technological sublime from the invention of railroads to computer networks, he argues that this cycle repeats time after time due to the “historical amnesia” that the sublime brings along. While every few years a new invention claims to be the future for real this time, the next one ends up picking up the similar hopes and anxieties its predecessor weaved — which is why the world spun just fine without GenAI only yesterday; and now your kebab shop’s menu has worms and tentacles in it. Sentiments like “how did people write essays before GPT?” go viral every other week. Whenever you vent to someone about being stuck in a personal, intellectual, or creative endeavor, you often hear “did you ask chat?” as the no-brainer solution.

On the flip side of this shift is the “anti-AI” camp who opposes this sudden, unceremonious, and at times cultish shift in common sense, longing for a pre-GenAI mode of existence that did not have them double check every artwork they look at, every email they receive, and every video they like. What the two sides have in common, though, is that they both operate from a material reality where GenAI tools are common tools that are becoming individual and industry norms for artistry. This zeitgeist that features the struggle between the “Luddite” and the “meat proxy” stances is the base that Niu Lai (牛来) (2026) spawns into. The film’s title roughly translates to “the ox/bull is coming”, which the director, Xin Yumeng, picked for good omen as he started making the film in 2021—the Year of the Ox.

Made by Xin and his mother, Sun Lifang, on a reported budget below $1200, the movie earned a mere $40 to an audience of 8 viewers on the day of its release. Upon receiving complaints about the poor quality of the film, 241 of the 245 theaters playing the movie withdrew it; Niu Lai set the record for the lowest box office performance of this year’s animation films. However, the amateurishness and the campiness of the animation gave the film an unexpected vitality, turning the little calf who was meant to be a representation for children’s struggles into a lolcow for adult audiences. These memes led to a surge in demand that took Niu Lai’s record for the lowest box performing animation of the year and turned it into the most profitable film of all time in the matter of two weeks.

This financial abundance, or milking, is the second layer that deepens the “bull” symbol that brings about a pun with its similarity to the Chinese term for a rising market — the “bull market”. People recognized the pun, and started seeing the film for abundance and luck, which, combined with the good omen of the Year of the Bull, parallels the financial fortune that followed the film’s rough start. With a box office gross of over $8.7 million, Niu Lai achieved a return of at least 20,000 times its budget, becoming the most profitable film of all time by dethroning the 2025 American horror indie film Obsession’s $750,000 budget against a gross of over $500 million.

The title that complements the film both as a lolcow milked for content, and a financial anomaly for the books, also contains the bittersweet compromise made in the way — as “the coming of the bull” promises an arrival that cannot arrive. Conceived before GenAI was popularized, yet spawned in the middle of it, Niu Lai’s text comes out stillborn. The bull’s story that was unable to be heard due to its technical and financial impossibilities is then heard only through them, moralizing Niu Lai into the beacon of hope within an AI debate it is uninformed of. Its “crude visuals” that would have been considered a mere flaw before are now, informed of the debate, "a testament to the irreplaceable value of emotional and physical toil”, mirroring the “concrete struggles of creation itself” (Park, 2026). The film, although uninformed of AI, is now named an “antidote” to “AI’s standardized aesthetics,” the film’s “non-industrial authenticity” and “clumsy sincerity” .

The praise for the director’s resilience and efforts in the face of financial impossibilities is indeed deserved. Xin had to convince his family to take on this endeavor that he is completely untrained in; he had to learn new skills and mediums that his background in landscape design did not quite cover; and he used second-hand equipment that often came broken from the get go. Although the production was mentally and financially challenging, Xin told himself that “there is no other way,” that he has to “do this thing.” However, unlike the anti-AI stance that has been attributed to this film, the director does not moralize the lack of AI assistance in the film; he simply says that there was no such technology available at the time.

Another romanticization is labor of love over AI. This project is not only a labor of love, but also love in the form of labor — collaborative work becoming the medium for love to manifest and evolve through familial and societal struggles. Initially, Xin’s family thought he was crazy for taking on this enterprise at his age (30, now 35), and were not optimistic about his chances of finishing and releasing the film. Once his mother got involved with the script, the two had ideological, directive and creative differences that led to conflict. The “terrifying process in terms of brain strength” caused Xin to break down often, leading to fights and arguments with his family, and him “slapping the table”. However, regardless of these differences and conflicts, Xin realized that he needed to learn from his parents’ experience in society, workplace, and interpersonal communication to enrich the movie. In parallel, the family supported their son through the process until the very end.

While the narrative of a mother’s sacrifice to bring his son’s dream come true played a big role in the redemption of this film’s reception, I believe it dismisses Sun’s labor and vision as mere motherly impulse. For instance, she learned how to write and compose from scratch as a 60-year-old “ordinary employee of a state-owned enterprise”. The closing track of Niu Lai, embedding Xin’s name in the lyrics, is about the hope for “when the wind is gentle and the rain is soft, when sprouts break through the soil”. Conflicts about parental expectations, future anxieties, and generational differences are expressed through music in this tune, which is the most heartfelt element in the movie.

Thus, the conditions are not praised for human creativity over artificial generation. Because, considering all of this information, there is a universe where Xin decided to take on this challenge a couple years later. Perhaps an “‘easy shortcut’ that skips the burdensome laborious, and hence inherently fulfilling process of human ideation, imagination, and production” would have seemed to be the better option to the director (Galip and Lupinacci, 2025). Considering that his daring to release a not perfectly polished picture brought him allegations of fraud, had people coming to his address to check if the family were legit, and turn his passion into mouthpieces for either cruel bullying or displaced praise — could we have blamed him, or any amateur, for biting into the forbidden fruit? Are gambling on second-hand equipment and having breakdowns over lack of skill the kind of moral milestones that any amateur can live through?

When we praise Niu Lai for not involving AI in its making, do we pose creativity as something in spite of AI, and not as its own mood? When we romanticize the negation of AI, do we romanticize the class related struggles and impossibilities the creators endured because they had no alternatives available to them? Do we turn creativity into a socioeconomic category that some are lucky enough to be born into, but most must grind and struggle to fit? Is Niu Lai the representative for human creativity, or a past piece that does not quite fit the contemporary puzzle that juxtaposes all endeavors in opposition to AI?

Actually, what even is Niu Lai about?

The film starts with a newborn calf struggling to walk while his peers were “born standing up”. Exhausted from trying, Niu Lai drifts off to a dreamscape that is entirely void except for him and the skylark talking to him. This dream is not of a new place or an idealized destination for him to take refuge in, but the utter negation of his everyday reality. This represents Niu Lai’s intrinsic disconnect with his herd — and his world. Even when he lacks the experience and imagination to speculate an alternative life, he feels deeply that this one isn't for him. Yet, whenever he tries to stray from his reality, his little adventures with others are interrupted by someone, often his mother, yelling out his name for the little calf to come back.

While the plot continues to involve confrontations, conflicts, and losses, the ending shows that it all had been Niu Lai’s dream. After seeing his mother sacrifice herself to the wolves in order to protect him, Niu Lai wakes up from his nap replenished, and ready to stand up on his feet — and with his mother patiently waiting by his side. Just as the film’s poster reads “a cow can rehearse its life—in dreams,” Niu Lai “becomes a fearless warrior who is not afraid of death” through the influences and experimentations in his dream —all of that without getting a single scratch. This is the exact exploratory, therapeutic, and bonding benefits of art that make creation a worthy endeavor regardless of one’s skill level or available tools.

A shot from the film that signals this theme of becoming through art would have never come to light had the process been outsourced to AI. Xin was not trained in animation but landscape design; so he made the film on an architecture software, likely SketchUp, which is the reason for this pixelated shadow of Niu Lai.

On an aesthetic level, these pixels break the fourth wall and emphasize the amateur artifice of the production, an artist creating in a medium that is alien to him — yet still pushing through. Making art might take a toll on your body and psychology; it might be a “terrible process in terms of brain strength” that gives you breakdowns and fights with your family. However, creation can detach whatever bothers you from yourself, turn your metaphysical torment into a tangible toy to prod and play with without consequence. It provides a medium removed from reality that allows real feelings and visions to resolve. This is how Niu Lai starts out as a cow with insecurities and problems with his herd, and grows into “a fearless warrior who is not afraid of death”. This is how the conflicts within the family resolve despite personal and generational differences, as artistic collaboration provides a platform that allows ideals and fears to be explored without real stakes, or the involvement of one’s pride and agenda.

On a psychoanalytical level, this shot reminds me of the Jungian “shadow”, which refers to the parts of the psyche that are repressed, pushed, and forgotten. However, instead of treating the self as a metaphysical mystery, this image deconstructs the shadow as the sum of blunt building blocks that signify traceable, evolving, and material occurrences. Your shadow is not an all absorbing blob situated in a space impossible to reach, but it is the sun shining on your body of becoming, imprinting your imperfect silhouette on grass. Your consciousness is not your abstract dictator, but it is your shade within nature.

These pixels also reminded me of my friend Ata’s filming and talking about his Lego creations for a handful of people when he was a kid. The unabashed clumsy creativity reminded me of the time I free-styled an entire novel by hand at the age 8 without an outline, Scrivener, or any knowledge about plot structures. Finally, the film’s ability to transfer its mood and message through language barriers reminded me of the time a friend of mine rapped the tracks he made during COVID lockdown to me in a language I don’t speak, which he’d only shown to his mother until then.

This is why I do not find Niu Lai to be an anti AI-slop icon, but rather a time capsule from an absurdly recent past. Its authenticity is not one of revolt that treats creativity as a moral virtue, but a rupture in the digital sublime and the historical amnesia that reclaims creation as our natural, inevitable mode of being.
