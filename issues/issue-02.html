issue 2 / August 28

this chinese cattle still serve their milk the old fashioned way: notes on niu l(ai)

Niu Lai’s authenticity is not in spite of AI — but an aura that precedes it. It is a rupture in the digital sublime and historical amnesia; Niu Lai is a glint of deslopification that reclaims creativity as our natural mode of living. The way it has always been.

Disclaimer: I could only watch a bootleg of the film with unofficial subtitles, and I had to rely on auto-translation for a lot of the materials I quoted. My twice removed viewing and researching of the film means this newsletter is prone to have mistakes in it, but that is okay. Niu Lai wouldn’t want me to do it any other way.

Only yesterday the world spun just fine without GAI; now my kebab shop’s menu has worms and tentacles in it. Sentiments like “how did people write essays before GPT?” go viral every other day. Whenever I vent to someone, whether it be about therapy costs, everyday tasks, or my frustration with a punchline that I just can’t get right for a standup routine, I risk being told did you ask chat? It often comes with a tone that assumes AI to be a no-brainer, that I must be silly for not outsourcing my challenges to AI.

It’s like no one can do, or should do, anything anymore. Which raises the question of weren’t we just doing things a couple of years ago? This sudden, unceremonious, and cultish turn to AI as the new common sense is due to the “historical amnesia” that occurs whenever a new technology comes around and claims that to be the future. For real this time (Mosco 8). This documented pattern from the invention of electricity to computers can explain the shift, while the obnoxiousness of AI hype can explain the passionate countermovement.

This divided zeitgeist is why Niu Lai (牛来) (2026) by Xin Yumeng and his mother Sun Lifang, created over 5 years on a reported budget below $1200, is the discussion of the week. The film’s title roughly translates to “the ox/bull is coming”, which the director picked for good omen as it was the Year of the Ox (2021) when he started making it (观察者网). This seemingly inconsequential title ended up representing several aspects of the film, albeit with some tragicomic reversals and twists.

Firstly, due to the amateurishness and the campiness of the animation, the little calf who was meant to be a representation for children’s struggles ended up a lolcow for adult audiences. In the matter of two weeks, the same memes took Niu Lai from the lowest performing animation box office opening to the most profitable movie of all time. This financial abundance, or milking of the “bull”, parallels the Chinese term for a rising market — the “bull market” (Tauber).

From children’s hero to adults’ lolcow, and box office flop to a market anomaly that raised the value of “bull” related stocks, it is no surprise that the third layer of “Niu Lai” is ironic too, as the title promises a coming that cannot arrive. The coming of age narrative cannot walk on its own, but is dragged to be the “antidote” to “AI’s standardized aesthetics” due to its “non-industrial authenticity” and “clumsy sincerity” (德闻; Tauber). Conceived before generative artificial was popularized, yet spawned in the middle of an AI debate it is uninformed of, Niu Lai’s text comes out stillborn.

Considering the hardships Xin bore for years, such as learning the craft from scratch to having to use second-hand hardware that often came broken, it is not wrong to praise Niu Lai for its resilience. Its “crude visuals” are not a flaw but "a testament to the irreplaceable value of emotional and physical toil”, mirroring the “concrete struggles of creation itself” (Park). Though, the director does not politicize or moralize the absence of AI tools in the film. He bluntly says that there was no artificial intelligence at the time he started making the movie — which means that had he acted on this idea a couple years later, we might have never gotten Niu Lai as his Niu Lai. Had he searched how to get into animation in 2026, he would have been bombarded with “AI tools” that offer an “‘easy shortcut’ that skips the burdensome laborious, and hence inherently fulfilling process of human ideation, imagination, and production.” (Galip and Lupinacci 4)

This pre-AI mode of existence that did not have any shortcut, or the pressure to use the shortcut, or the impossibility of avoiding the shortcut that is embedded everywhere, is key to the aura of authenticity in Niu Lai. Xin’s “you have to do this thing” mentality despite the “relatively painful process” is not an active revolt, but a return to the normal normal — reclamation of the natural course of creation until now.

Another aspect that popularized Niu Lai is the five-year mother-son collaboration behind it. While the narrative of a mother’s sacrifice to bring his son’s dream come true played a big role in the redemption of this film’s reception, I believe it dismisses Sun’s labor and vision as mere motherly impulse. To me, this project is not only a labor of love, but also love in the form of labor — collaborative work becoming the medium for love to manifest and evolve through familial and societal struggles.

Although the family supported Xin until the very end, they first thought he was crazy for taking on this enterprise at his age. They were not optimistic about his chances of finishing and releasing a project outside of his major. During the making of the film, the “terrifying process in terms of brain strength” caused Xin to break down often, leading to fights and arguments with his family, and him “slapping the table”. Moreover, once his mother got involved with the script, the two had ideological, directive and creative differences that led to conflict.

However, being “only 30 years old”, Xin needed to learn from his parents’ experience in society, workplace, and interpersonal communication if he wanted to enrich the narrative. Artistic collaboration provided a platform that allowed ideals and fears to be explored without real stakes, or the involvement of one’s pride and agenda. Thus, what might ring condescending or controlling in conversation tunes to mediation in art, which can be heard in the soundtrack composed and sung by Sun. Her taking up of the task as an “ordinary employee of a state-owned enterprise” turned her parental worries and expectations into a song about the hope for “when the wind is gentle and the rain is soft, when sprouts break through the soil” that embeds Xin’s name in the lyrics.

Such reconciliation of family conflict and social alienation through creative play is the heart of Niu Lai. The movie starts with him struggling to walk as a newborn calf while his peers were “born standing up”. Exhausted from trying, Niu Lai drifts off to a dreamscape that is entirely void except for the figures floating in the abyss. He doesn’t dream of a new place or an idealized destination to take refuge in — but the utter negation of his everyday reality. This represents Niu Lai’s intrinsic disconnect with his herd and his world. Even when he lacks the experience and imagination to speculate an alternative life, he still feels deeply that this one isn't for him.

Whenever Niu Lai strays too far from home and tries to take on an adventure, his experimentation is interrupted by someone, most often his mother, yelling out his name for him to come back. However, these restrictions come from a place of love and duty for one’s child, which is shown in the mother’s sacrificing herself to the wolves stalking their herd in order to protect Niu Lai. When he grows up to avenge his mother, and is about to put his body on the line to protect their landscape, Niu Lai suddenly wakes up from his nap.

It was all a dream through which, in Xin’s words, the newborn calf “becomes a fearless warrior who is not afraid of death” without getting a single scratch. This is the exploratory, therapeutic, and bonding effect of art that would have never manifested had the process been left to AI. Through the challenges of imagination and play, Niu Lai wakes up replenished, with his mother on his side — finally ready to stand on his feet for the first time.

Had Xin taken a shortcut, my favorite frame from the film would have never come to life either. Because he was not trained in animation but landscape design, he made the movie on an architecture software, which led to this pixelated shadow of Niu Lai. It reminded me of my friend Ata’s Youtube videos from his childhood where he babbles about his Lego creations for a handful of people. Or the time a friend of mine, tipsy, decided to rap tracks he made during COVID lockdown to me in a language I don’t speak, which he’d only shown to his mother until then. Or when I was 8 and decided to free-style an entire novel on a whim — no outline, no Scrivener, not even a computer.

So, to me, this film is not the moral upstanding of human imperfection in the face of generated coherence, but rather a time capsule from a painfully recent past. Niu Lai’s authenticity is not in spite of AI — but an aura that precedes it. It is a rupture in the digital sublime and historical amnesia; Niu Lai is a glint of deslopification that reclaims creativity as our natural mode of living. The way it has always been.
