issue 3 / September 4

generated microdramas for generated micro lives

By removing both the worker and the artist out of the loop, AI-generated microdramas kill two birds with one stone for capital accumulation.

“Drama” pauses life, probes its contents, and rearranges the pieces in a new totality. If drama is inevitably cropped and curtailed, a “microdrama” is the slice of slice of life. Artificially generated microdramas, then, are the computational compressing of a readily compressed and recompressed moment — not even a slice³ of life, but an algorithmic estimation of what might entail slice² of life to a scrolling audience.

As a result, what used to be a “slice of life” is now the “negation of life”. Thermodynamically, “life” can’t have vanished into thin air but must have been valorized somewhere, repurposed into something — just as “craft” is alienated into “commodity”; “life” is superseded by “profit” as the pulse of performance; and “representation” is replaced by “spectacle” as the function of drama. While the culprit is inevitably the capital, the time of crime is not very easy to pinpoint, as there has never been a time when the ruling class did not impede creativity. Class struggle has never not imprinted on art.

Only the power gap between the artist and the capitalist distribution fluctuated, which rapidly multiplied after the industrial revolution. Guy Debord theorized our post-Fordism existence to be a “pseudo-world” in which “commodity became the colonizing principle of social life” in a web much wider than media. Theodor W. Adorno maps out this within the “culture industry” in the 1940s, arguing that although the “culture monopolies” seem independent and progressive, even the most powerful broadcasting company relies on the electrical industry, and film on the banks — which means whatever makes it on our screens have already been tamed and flattened by capital.

This synthesis is why although the theorists I quote lived a century or two before the invention of “AI-generated alpha billionaire CEO romance”, they can still help trace a trend in media that is not disrupted or replaced but only amplified by GAI. While the absurdities of AI-slop trick us into thinking that we are so cooked, that our moment is a unique struggle, that this is the actual end of times — AI slop is simply the logical progression of capitalist mode of production. What distinguishes the affect and aesthetic of GAI as specific to our era is the “abject cringe” evoked by being bombarded with objects that are unabashedly inhuman.

The foregrounding of capital and the removal of its subject is unusually grotesque; the replacement of “capitalism with a human face” by “capitalism in your face” post-Cold War is at its most transparent. GAI makes it infinitely easier to not only flaunt the victory of the capital, but also “flood the zone with bullshit in order to erode the intellectual foundations” for resisting it. The “absence of humans” is “a feature, not a bug, of AI art”, as it removes the artist who historically leaned left, and speeds the process to “liberate capital from labor for good”. By removing both the worker and the artist out of the loop, AI-generated microdramas kill two birds with one stone for capital accumulation. Therefore, by studying their production, distribution, and consumption, we can trace how classical descriptions of capitalism manifests in contemporary conditions through studying the phenomena of AI-generated microdramas.

First, what justifies and popularizes these tools of serialized plagiarism is what Karl Marx diagnosed as “commodity fetishism”. The term describes the detachment of a commodity from the material reality, and the replacement of its tangible use-value with a new mystical value that is related to the labor behind it, but removed further from it with every iteration. (For instance, a movie ticket abstracts every step of labor that went into making that film, every layer of surplus value extracted from the workers who made the movie and the ticket. Both the time you get to sit in the cinema, and the piece of paper that permits you to sit act as commodities that can relate to one another independently of the material reality of their production. They insist on a value of their own beyond their exchange value, which is how fetishism justifies the mystical price it demands as fact.)

Generative artificial intelligence is the amplification of commodity fetishism. AI extracts from whatever it can get into its possession, and abstracts these resources in untraceable, unarguable, and unquestionable code; AI’s process of churning and spitting is the fetishization of fetishism. Through this abstraction, corporations can reframe the hard work of the artists as a mythological, gatekept resource that is “talent”. They can claim to “democratize” creativity for the masses and “lower the barriers to entry” through GAI. Even recalling last week’s Niu Lai (2026), we can concur that the barrier to make art is as short as one’s ego. What might require certain conditions is the mastery of craft.

In fear that people will figure out these conditions to be the fulfillment of basic needs, leisurely time, and humane working conditions; capital offers “salvation in granting expression to the masses—but on no account of granting them rights.” Neoliberalism provides the masses new canvases and shiny tools to express frustration “while leaving intact the property relations which they strive to abolish.” That is why the subject believes the emancipation of “people who can’t draw but have good ideas” is not through the conditions that will make improvement possible, but in a politicized claim to “create images”.

Plus, if this is the result of the democratization of making art, did the whole world take a vote on bullshit? Microdrama, a genre that is already preformed and degraded by the requirements of short content, is infinitely, indistinguishably more degenerate with AI’s taking over the industry. Because what is prioritized is profit and not artistry, AI speeds the process by rapidly reproducing popular tropes “billionaire boyfriend”, which promotes the status-quo in disguise of escapism. The “billionaire CEO boyfriend” that we dismiss as silly serves ideology by dramatizing class conflict as a trivial, interpersonal, and natural beat of life. The “billionaire” is depicted as flawed due to being a distant and confusing lover — not because he is exploiting the labor of thousands. This character too is fetishized into a commodity who we do not question the making of — he is a billionaire, just because. That is romantic, just because.

On the other hand, the actors whose images were stolen for these videos do not have as stable and self-assured connections to their work. The most extreme cases involve early career actors who signed up for these gigs in 2024-2025, when microdramas were a booming industry that made up 28% of the casting calls in LA. Their contracts did not include protection against their likeness being used time and time again for profit; their union had already sided with the studios in 2023; and their courts do not care about intellectual property if it doesn’t concern corporations. Thus, the labor of these actors who starred in and got paid for a microdrama is re-alienated and re-exploited thousands of times. Their consent is not only violated in volume, but also in the manner in which their images were manipulated, such as actresses who never agreed to nudity or intimacy seeing pop-up ads of their likeness getting stripped and grabbed by the likeness of a man.

The viewers who consume the estrangement of the actors are as, if not more, alienated in their existence. Microdrama producers say “the world is in crisis, and people are looking for dopamine”; and the fans “regularly described micro dramas as a way for them to relax and escape the stress of current events or difficult personal situations.” Both groups imply “escape” to be a self-explanatory, common sense need without naming what they are exactly escaping from. Their exploitation has been cemented as the inevitable truth of life, and their “efforts to escape it have finally transformed the ancient concept of fate” into one now that is decided by capital — which commodifies the need to escape into a product for “escapism”.

What makes these products of escapism different from their previous versions in traditional media is the efficiency at which they can prey on the audience’s weakness for “emotional realism, narrative involvement and algorithmic personalization.” For instance, even the most commercial and median movie is at least curated for a crowd and not specifically for you, unlike AI-slop that spews out highly individualized versions of the same tropes. Even a Marvel film is experienced from a big screen among other people who breathe, react, and anchor you in physical reality, whereas your blanket theatre and all-consuming screen not only absorbs you, but gives you no reason to detach from it as no one is witnessing your sloth.

It is not that you are stupid or you have a “shortened attention span” (which pathologizes an individual person for what is the symptom of systemic neglect). It is that you are estranged, and capitalism coerces you to grow dependent on your oppression. Even as you call it “ironic”, or watch it “with your brain turned off”, and ask others to let you “enjoy things”, your involvement is how the “spectacle self-validates its continued existence with the continual affirmation of its mode of production”.

Your indulgence in escapism only enhances the proficiency of your estrangement, which turns you to seek more escapism from the one you are trying to escape. As “escapism” is a continued product, escape itself will never be possible, regardless of how much unreality you get to consume: “The accumulation of satisfaction becomes a gilded poverty, but it cannot transcend it.” While existence diminishes into microdrama, you keep generating life for the structure that deprives you of it.
