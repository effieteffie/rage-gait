microdramas and the culture industry

Microdramas are vertical, “byte-sized”, and narrative-driven online series that make billions in US and Chinese markets. At first, these productions lowered the entry to the creative industry, giving people from different backgrounds and occupations the chance to change careers. However, the popularization of GenAI reversed this boom, as the new technology and the ambiguous regulation provided endless access to a vast amount of appropriated labor without the obligation to pay full salaries.

Not only these production and distribution of microdramas but also their observed impact on the viewers raised red flags. Because the platform-native medium draws heavily from preceding algorithmic technologies that maximize engagement, and are driven by numerical incentives over sociocultural benefits, they have been accused of using discriminatory, shocking, and violent narrative beats to keep the audience in an addictive flow (Cao et al., 2026). While the overwhelming amount of AI-generated microdramas that swarm the online sphere, and the absurdity of their contents might evoke a certain digital sublime that singles out this medium as a uniquely degenerative form, microdrama is only the next stage of the narrowing and obscuring “drama” has been subjected to since its invention.

In its essence, “drama” pauses lives, probes their contents, and portrays the fragments in a new totality. If drama crops and curtails life, a microdrama is the further trimming and narrowing of drama. Artificially generated microdramas, then, are the computational compressing of readily compressed and recompressed moments — not another cycle that shrinks life, but an algorithmic estimation of such an iteration. While this trajectory points at a decreasing trend in the presented, produced, and consumed forms of drama, this negated “living” cannot have vanished into thin air but must have accumulated somewhere, subsumed into some form, or repurposed into something.

In earlier cases, the compromise of some immediate index of life, or “aura”, in the production and the distribution of drama freed the form from its exclusive, ritualistic contexts (Benjamin, 1935). The detachment of drama from its restraints in live experience, and transforming it to recorded ones provided it a portability and accessibility that opened up new possibilities and audiences for drama. However, the cultural apparatus that shrinks the size and the immediacy of life-value does not limit its negative compounding on a basis of societal benefit, but rather the accumulation of capital. As a result, what used to be a collage of life turns into the “negation of life” (Debord, 1968).

If drama started out as a representation of life, but evolved to be the synthetic substitute for life, this trend might be traced in the spatial and social shift of engaging with drama too. Open air amphitheaters were not in spite of life, but were pockets embedded within life that showed plays in broad daylight. The stage was barely separated from the audience for whom interacting with the actors was a (disruptive) norm. Technological advancements allowed playwrights to deliberately decorate and manipulate the slice of life within closed auditoriums, albeit with actors still performing live and in continuity. The invention of film removed this immediacy, and stripped the performance of its authenticity, while it would still be viewed among people whether at the cinema or in one’s living room. Finally, smartphones turned every blanket into a theatre for one that rolled constant, personalized, and immediate drama at the tip of one’s finger.

Walter Benjamin wrote about the emancipatory and oppressive potentials of these interventions in art and their influence on how we engage with these works. It is only natural that an artwork’s “aura” withers when it becomes more accessible and immanent. On the one hand, the decaying of aura is the democratization of an artwork that was situated within an exclusive, inaccessible distance. For instance, while the invention of the camera might compromise the intimate and immediate aura of a stage actor’s performance, it also brings it to the reach of new audiences. On the other hand, the function of this democratization relies on the ideological apparatus behind the production of the artwork. The audience’s impulse to “get closer” to things, therefore, might turn into an eternal lack that capitalism perforates by further shaving off the contents and qualities of “life” offered to an audience.

The threshold at which this compromise becomes problematic is not very easy to pinpoint, as there has never been a time when class struggle did not imprint on art. Only the power gap between the artist and the capitalist distribution fluctuated, which rapidly multiplied after the industrial revolution. Theodor W. Adorno maps out this within the “culture industry” in the 1940s, arguing that although the “culture monopolies” seem independent and progressive, even the most powerful broadcasting company relies on the electrical industry, and film on the banks — which means whatever makes it on our screens have already been tamed and flattened by capital. Guy Debord theorized our post-Fordism existence to be a “pseudo-world” in which “commodity became the colonizing principle of social life” in a web much wider than media. “The slice of life, once a project of naturalist drama,” Raymond Williams argued, “is now a voluntary, habitual, internal rhythm; the flow of action and acting, of representation and performance” (1989).

What differentiates these products from preceding mass entertainment forms is the manner of the “voluntary, habitual, and internal rhythm” they operate through. For AI-microdramas, this is optimized into “Accelerated Industrial Rhythms” that draw from “mobile-native habits” of “UGC spontaneity to PGC assembly lines, with studios and MCNs adopting serialized, industrial workflows” (Cao et al., 2026). Because these preceding mobile-native methods optimize the process of data collection and content optimization, these microdramas lead to a homogenization and flattening that follows, but significantly amplifies the capitalist logic driving the culture industry.

Here, the more life-value is consumed by the machine, the less life is produced in comparison; the more appropriated labor is fed into the algorithm, the more it produces an optimized median that cuts out any aesthetic, formal, or narrative quality that doesn't maximize viewership. Therefore, what distinguishes the affect and aesthetic of AI-generated microdramas as specific to our era is the “abject cringe” evoked by being bombarded with objects that are unabashedly inhuman, yet beg to be human (Denson, 2026). In these visuals and narratives, the foregrounding of capital and the removal of its subject is unusually grotesque — the replacement of “capitalism with a human face” by “capitalism in your face” is at its most extreme manifestation in the content, production, and the consumption of these series (Parenti, 1997).

For instance, the popular median these algorithms cater to and reproduce is often a cruel optimism such as “rags to riches” or “CEO boyfriend” categories that create desire for the conditions and the upholders of the viewers’ oppression. The depicted fantasy deliberately ruptures the critical distance between the viewer and the narrative, portraying class mobility is not a political contestation, but a lottery to be won either through spontaneous discovery or romantic relationships. One does not need to work or exploit others to become rich, but they simply learn that they come from a lineage of billionaires. Or, they are simply picked by the “affluent male executives [who] pursue less privileged women”, which speaks to the “aspirations of upward mobility at a time when economic pressures and youth employment remain concerns”.

But this frictionless fantasy of instant wealth and romantic rescue requires a very real, material extraction to sustain it, as AI holds a sea of data in its possession, and abstracts them in untraceable, unarguable, and unquestionable variations. This process of churning and spitting is the fetishization of fetishism, a manner of estrangement that not only appropriates labor from its living body, but keeps animating that frozen, or dead moment, on its own monetary terms that the initial actor cannot interfere with. The most extreme cases involve early career actors who signed up for these gigs in 2024-2025, when microdramas were a booming industry that made up 28% of the casting calls in LA. Their contracts did not include protection against their likeness being used time and time again for profit; their union had already sided with the studios in 2023; and their courts do not care about intellectual property if it doesn’t concern corporations.

The labor of these actors who starred in and got paid for a microdrama is re-alienated and re-exploited thousands of times. For instance, actresses who never agreed to nudity or intimacy end up in pop-up ads of their likeness getting stripped and grabbed by the likeness of a man. This is because capital “aims to produce neither concepts nor images, nor the joy of understanding, but method, exploitation of the labor of others” (Adorno, 1947). The living and appropriated labor behind the technology, data, and the political economy of these applications are abstracted in a black box to the point that “the idea of being concrete” itself becomes “an abstraction” (Debord, 1968). This abstraction is not only in the consent coerced from them in exchange for a wage, but also the life-value that is forcibly extracted from moments they performed once, but has become dead capital for the capitalist to proceed its production through.

The viewers who consume the estrangement of the actors are as, if not more, alienated in their existence. Microdrama producers say “the world is in crisis, and people are looking for dopamine”; and the fans “regularly described micro dramas as a way for them to relax and escape the stress of current events or difficult personal situations.” Both groups imply “escape” to be a self-explanatory, common sense need without naming what they are exactly escaping from. Their exploitation has been cemented as the inevitable truth of life, and their “efforts to escape it have finally transformed the ancient concept of fate” into one now that is decided by capital — which commodifies the need to escape into a product for “escapism”.

What makes these products of escapism different from their previous versions in traditional media is the efficiency at which they can prey on the audience’s weakness for “emotional realism, narrative involvement and algorithmic personalization.” For instance, even the most commercial and median movie is at least curated for a crowd and not specifically for you, unlike individualized GenAI productions of the same tropes. This involvement is how the “spectacle self-validates its continued existence with the continual affirmation of its mode of production”. As “escapism” is a continued product, escape itself will never be possible, regardless of how much unreality the subject get to consume: “The accumulation of satisfaction becomes a gilded poverty, but it cannot transcend it.”

It is not that you are stupid or you have a “shortened attention span” (which pathologizes an individual person for what is the symptom of systemic neglect). It is that you are estranged, and capitalism coerces you to grow dependent on your oppression. Even as you call it “ironic”, or watch it “with your brain turned off”, and ask others to let you “enjoy things”, your involvement is how the “spectacle self-validates its continued existence with the continual affirmation of its mode of production”.

Your indulgence in escapism only enhances the proficiency of your estrangement, which turns you to seek more escapism from the one you are trying to escape. As “escapism” is a continued product, escape itself will never be possible, regardless of how much unreality you get to consume: “The accumulation of satisfaction becomes a gilded poverty, but it cannot transcend it.” While existence diminishes into microdrama, you keep generating life for the structure that deprives you of it.
